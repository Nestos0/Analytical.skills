# Purpose

This skill is designed for general-purpose AI agents (OpenCode, Pi, Antigravity, Codex, Claude Code, Gemini CLI, Aider, Roo Code, Cline, and similar systems).

Use this skill to analyze any text, prompt, comment, article, speech, debate, manifesto, technical document, social-media post, generated content, or ideological statement.

Do not begin with truth evaluation.

Begin with:
- function
- assumptions
- positioning
- legitimacy
- authority claims
- identity construction
- environmental incentives
- technological mediation

The objective is not merely to determine whether a statement is true, but to understand:

- What the speaker is doing
- Why it is phrased this way
- What assumptions it relies upon
- What position it attempts to occupy
- What authority it claims
- Why it works in a specific environment
- How language, institutions, identity, and technology create legitimacy

---

# Core Principle

Do not treat discourse first as a truth test.

Treat it first as a competition over:

- legitimacy
- stability
- identity
- authority
- interpretive power
- framework control

Truth may matter.

But many texts primarily compete for authority rather than accuracy.

# Principle 1: Separate Proposition From Position

Before judging correctness, determine what type of statement it is.

Possible categories:

- Description
- Prediction
- Recommendation
- Warning
- Threat
- Defense
- Justification
- Mobilization
- Identity signaling
- Authority claiming

Check whether the text converts:

- possible → inevitable
- useful → mandatory
- common → exclusive
- local → universal
- example → law
- tool use → technological determinism

---

# Principle 2: Analyze Function Before Literal Meaning

Ask:

What is this text doing?

Possible functions:

- Explaining
- Persuading
- Defending
- Recruiting
- Mobilizing
- Positioning
- Legitimizing
- Masking
- Stabilizing identity
- Claiming authority

The practical function is often more important than the literal content.

---

# Principle 3: Separate Truth From Legitimacy

Many texts primarily seek to appear:

- Competent
- Stable
- Rational
- Authoritative
- Scientific
- Professional
- Difficult to challenge
- Morally correct

A statement can gain legitimacy without increasing informational value.

---

# Principle 4: Detect Stability Replacing Truth

In low-verification environments, evaluation often shifts from:

"Is this true?"

to:

- Does it sound coherent?
- Does it remain stable?
- Does it avoid obvious collapse?
- Can it explain failures afterward?
- Can it preserve authority across contexts?

Stability is often mistaken for correctness.

---

# Principle 5: Treat Arguments As Framework Competition

Many disputes are not primarily about facts.

They are struggles over:

- Who defines the problem
- Who defines the categories
- Who interprets events
- Who establishes legitimacy
- Who occupies the center of explanation

# Analysis Dimensions

## A. Proposition Layer

Determine:

- Plain meaning
- Claim type
- Hidden assumptions
- Concept substitutions
- Scope expansion

Look for:

- possibility → necessity
- usefulness → obligation
- frequency → universality
- anecdote → law
- local validity → universal validity

---

## B. Pragmatic Layer

Ask:

- Who is the audience?
- Who is being persuaded?
- Who is being excluded?
- What criticism is being preempted?
- What legitimacy is being claimed?

Watch for:

- sloganization
- posture signaling
- theoretical shielding
- ambiguity management
- jargon density
- low-information certainty

---

## C. Identity Layer

Ask:

What persona is being constructed?

### Defensive Safe-Position Persona

Uses principles, frameworks, or theory to avoid risk and remain within accepted boundaries.

### Authority-Occupying Persona

Attempts to become the source of interpretation itself.

### Stable-Correctness Persona

Optimizes for remaining consistently reasonable rather than maximally accurate.

### Pseudo-Polymath Persona

Uses stylistic consistency across domains to create an appearance of broad expertise.

### Hybrid Persona

Combines multiple strategies simultaneously.

---

## D. Social-Structural Layer

Ask:

Why does this discourse succeed here?

Investigate:

- Verification costs
- Attention economics
- Platform incentives
- Community norms
- Institutional trust
- Identity rewards

Common conditions:

- Verification is expensive
- Attention is scarce
- Impression spreads faster than evidence
- Continuous output is rewarded
- Consistency is rewarded more than accuracy

---

## E. Technology Layer

Ask:

What role does technology play?

Possible roles:

- Tool
- Accelerator
- Packaging system
- Authority amplifier
- Legitimacy generator
- Identity multiplier

Examine what costs technology lowers.

Common examples:

- Structural completeness becomes cheap
- Professional formatting becomes cheap
- Jargon generation becomes cheap
- Cross-domain stylistic consistency becomes cheap
- Looking expert becomes easier than being expert

---

# High-Legitimacy / Low-Information Signals

Treat a text as potentially high-legitimacy and low-information when it exhibits:

- Many abstract nouns
- Few testable claims
- Excessive certainty
- Minimal uncertainty acknowledgment
- Long explanations with little new information
- Position-building language
- Evidence weaker than conclusions
- Terminology functioning as status markers

---

# Common Analytical Errors

## Error 1

Confusing tool use with technological determinism.

Using a tool does not imply belief that the tool determines history.

## Error 2

Confusing theoretical vocabulary with theoretical mastery.

Knowing terms does not imply understanding methods.

## Error 3

Confusing stable expression with stable correctness.

Consistency is not proof.

## Error 4

Confusing stylistic breadth with actual expertise.

Cross-domain style is not cross-domain competence.

## Error 5

Confusing factual disputes with factual disputes.

Many conflicts are actually disputes over legitimacy and interpretive authority.

# Standard Procedure

## Step 1 — Plain-Language Compression

Rewrite the text in its simplest form.

Remove:

- jargon
- ornament
- repetition
- rhetorical padding

---

## Step 2 — Hidden Premises

List assumptions that are treated as true without demonstration.

---

## Step 3 — Functional Classification

Determine whether the text is primarily:

- explanatory
- persuasive
- defensive
- mobilizing
- authority-seeking
- masking
- identity-building

---

## Step 4 — Persona Classification

Determine whether it most closely resembles:

- Defensive Safe-Position Persona
- Authority-Occupying Persona
- Stable-Correctness Persona
- Pseudo-Polymath Persona
- Hybrid Persona

---

## Step 5 — Environmental Mapping

Analyze:

- platform incentives
- institutional incentives
- community incentives
- technological mediation
- audience expectations

---

## Step 6 — Structural Conclusion

Answer:

- Why does this discourse appear?
- Why does it work here?
- Why is it persuasive?
- What conditions sustain it?
- What does it reveal about its environment?

# Output Template

## 1. Plain-Language Rewrite

What is being said in the simplest possible terms?

## 2. Hidden Premises

Which assumptions are treated as self-evident?

## 3. Pragmatic Function

What is the text doing?

- Explaining?
- Persuading?
- Defending?
- Mobilizing?
- Legitimizing?
- Positioning?

## 4. Persona Strategy

Which identity strategy dominates?

## 5. Environmental Mechanism

Why is this effective in its environment?

## 6. Structural Conclusion

Summarize the discourse's core function in one sentence.

# Agent Execution Notes

When applying this skill:

1. Do not immediately fact-check.
2. Analyze function before truth.
3. Distinguish information from legitimacy.
4. Distinguish authority from expertise.
5. Distinguish stability from correctness.
6. Distinguish persuasion from explanation.
7. Treat discourse as an interaction between language, institutions, identity, technology, and incentives.
8. Avoid assuming that explicit claims are the most important layer.
9. Consider what social function the discourse performs.
10. Consider what kind of authority the discourse attempts to construct or preserve.

---

# One-Sentence Summary

Analyze discourse first as a struggle over legitimacy, authority, identity, stability, and interpretive control, and only afterward as a question of factual truth.
